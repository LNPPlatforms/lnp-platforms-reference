Why it matters

Confidence isn't the same as accuracy.

90% / 70%

Clinicians felt highly certain in 90% of ear infection diagnoses. Only 70% were true infections.2

26%

of antibiotic prescriptions in that study were potentially unnecessary.2

3x

The same child is up to three times as likely to get an antibiotic depending on which practice they visit.3

Without a tympanogram

"Looks red. Let's start amoxicillin."

  • Crying, wax and lighting decide the diagnosis
  • Fluid that's really there gets missed
  • Adults with sudden nerve hearing loss get antibiotics instead of an urgent ENT referral
With the easyTymp

"Type A, no fluid. This is viral."

  • An objective reading in about 10 seconds
  • Documented in the chart, billed under CPT 92567
  • Clear next step when it's time to refer
Quality scores

Protect your stewardship measures.

Payers grade clinics on avoiding unnecessary antibiotics. The red-ear prescription during a viral cold is one of the easiest to lose points on, and one of the easiest to fix.

HEDIS antibiotic measures

Fewer prescriptions for viral upper respiratory infections means better scores and fewer quality penalties.

Specialty guidance

The AAP holds that otitis media shouldn't be diagnosed with a normal tympanogram, and the AAO-HNS recommends tympanometry whenever fluid is in doubt.4

A habit your team keeps

Compassio's course and live webinars make "tympanogram first" part of every ear exam, not a device in a drawer.

ENT-backed

Know when to treat, and when to refer.

A normal tympanogram with sudden hearing loss in an adult points to nerve loss, not fluid. Patients have about two weeks to see an ENT for treatment that can help save their hearing.4 Your local ENT partner is part of the program, so those referrals have a home.
